# Navigation and flight planning: opening lesson

Status: DRAFT. Provisional South African subject; current syllabus mapping and competent review pending.

## Objective

Keep time, distance and reference frames consistent.

## Lesson

Navigation calculations are meaningful only when units and reference frames are consistent. In a simple constant-speed ground-track example, time equals distance divided by ground speed. Two kilometres is 2000 metres; at 5 metres per second, the calculated travel time is 400 seconds. That result excludes acceleration, turns, wind changes, reserve and every other omitted mission factor.

Heading describes the direction the aircraft is pointed. Ground track describes its movement over the ground. Wind can make these differ. A map line alone does not establish the heading, energy or clearance required to follow it.

Coordinate-reference systems give numerical coordinates geographic meaning. Degrees of latitude and longitude are angular units, not metres. Changing a layer's label is different from transforming its coordinate values. A display that aligns layers cannot by itself validate a measurement workflow.

In mapping work, independent checkpoints are held out from the fitting or adjustment process so they can provide an independent check. Keep their role separate from control points. A planning note should identify units, horizontal and vertical references, the information omitted and the checks needed before applying a calculation to a real operation.
